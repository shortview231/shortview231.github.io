AI in Education: From Sentiment to Evidence

The most useful change in this project was methodological: stop treating every dataset about AI and education as if it measures the same thing.

The problem

Similar topics do not guarantee comparable evidence.

Surveys can differ by population, geography, year, wording, sampling method, and even what they mean by “AI use.” Combining them too quickly can produce a cleaner-looking answer with weaker evidence behind it.

The correction

I began treating source comparability as a first-class analytical requirement rather than a cleanup step after data collection.

Four rules that improved the analysis

1 • Provenance

Know where every field came from.

Source, collection period, population, definitions, transformations, and known limitations need to remain traceable after data is joined or summarized.

2 • Comparability

Do not pool unlike measurements.

Two surveys discussing AI may still answer different questions. I now prefer a smaller compatible comparison over a larger misleading one.

3 • Confounders

Keep competing explanations visible.

Changes in employment, wages, learning outcomes, or attitudes can have many causes. AI exposure alone is not enough to establish causation.

4 • Claim strength

Match the conclusion to the evidence.

Descriptive results support descriptive claims. Correlations support association claims. Strong causal language requires much stronger identification.

What this demonstrates

Data analysis includes deciding what not to combine.

A useful analytical workflow is not measured only by how much data it can ingest. It should preserve enough context to tell when sources are incompatible, when an inference is weak, and what evidence would actually reduce uncertainty.
