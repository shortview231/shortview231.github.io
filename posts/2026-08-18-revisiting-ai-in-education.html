Returning to Where I Started: Revisiting AI in Education

Published August 18, 2026 · Research Notes · Data Analysis · Artificial Intelligence

A year ago, one of the first projects I chose to analyze publicly was the role of artificial intelligence in education.

At the time, my workflow looked completely different from the one I use today. I wasn't working with coding agents or automated research pipelines. I was still learning through manual implementation: discussing a problem with ChatGPT, getting commands or code, typing them into VS Code myself, seeing what happened, and working through the results.

That AI in Education project became one of my first experiences turning data analysis into something intended for a public audience.

Only a year later, I am returning to the same question from an entirely different place.

The Question Has Gotten Bigger

The original project was largely about understanding attitudes toward AI in education.

Today, I am interested in a much broader question:

What is actually happening as generative AI becomes part of education and everyday work?

There is no shortage of opinions.

Depending on where you look, AI is transforming education, destroying critical thinking, helping struggling students, enabling cheating, reducing teacher workloads, threatening jobs, improving accessibility, or somehow doing all of those things simultaneously.

I don't want to begin with any of those conclusions.

I want to begin with the evidence.

Building the Project Differently This Time

The biggest difference between my original project and this one isn't just the subject. It is the methodology.

Instead of relying on one dataset, I am building an evidence base from multiple sources and populations.

That includes student surveys, educator research, institutional data, labor-market information, AI exposure estimates, government statistics, and other sources that can help separate what people believe AI is doing from what we can actually observe happening.

Those are not necessarily the same thing.

For example, a survey may tell us that workers are afraid AI will eliminate jobs. That is important evidence about perception.

It does not, by itself, demonstrate that AI eliminated those jobs.

Likewise, a company announcing an AI initiative does not prove that subsequent layoffs were caused by AI.

The challenge is connecting claims to measurable outcomes while being careful about what the data can and cannot establish.

Research Should Be Allowed to Change Your Mind

One rule I want to maintain throughout this project is simple:

The conclusion does not get decided before the analysis.

I have my own expectations about what the data might show. Everyone does.

But a useful research process should be capable of proving those expectations wrong.

That means actively looking for counterexamples, conflicting datasets, alternative explanations, and evidence supporting multiple sides of the debate.

If AI appears associated with improved outcomes somewhere, I want to understand where and under what conditions.

If it appears associated with worse outcomes somewhere else, I want to understand that too.

And when the available evidence cannot support a causal conclusion, the answer should simply be:

We don't know yet.

That is still a useful finding.

Coming Back With a Different Workflow

There is something fitting about returning to this particular project only a year later.

The first time I worked on AI in Education, there were no coding agents in my workflow. I didn't know what Codex was. ChatGPT was primarily my conversational partner and teacher. We would work through a problem together, I would take the commands or code we developed, and I would manually implement them in VS Code.

That process taught me a lot because I had to touch nearly every part of the work myself.

Today, the relationship looks very different.

I still use conversation as the starting point, but now that conversation sits inside a much larger research and engineering workflow. Research projects can maintain journals, track datasets and sources, preserve methodology decisions, record unresolved questions, and move through defined stages. Coding agents can handle implementation work that previously required long stretches of manual input.

The interesting part is that the human role hasn't disappeared.

If anything, I've become more interested in the parts that shouldn't simply be automated: deciding whether evidence is convincing, questioning assumptions, recognizing when two datasets aren't actually comparable, and deciding what conclusions the evidence warrants.

In that sense, revisiting AI in Education is also an accidental experiment in something else:

How much can a human-AI research workflow evolve in a single year?

The AI tools changed.

My skills changed.

The workflow changed.

Now I get to return to the same subject and see whether those changes produce better research.

What Comes Next

Over the coming posts, I will share pieces of that process: datasets I find, methodological problems, unexpected results, competing explanations, visualizations, failed hypotheses, and eventually the conclusions that survive the evidence.

Some posts may be substantial analyses. Others may simply explore an interesting result, a research paper, a methodological problem, or a question that came up while working.

The goal isn't to make every post a finished research paper.

It's to make the process visible.

A year ago, AI in Education helped me learn how to turn data into a public-facing project.

Now I'm coming back to it with better tools, better questions, and a very different way of working.

I don't know exactly where the research will lead yet.

And honestly, that's what makes it exciting: letting the data lead me somewhere I may not have expected to go.
