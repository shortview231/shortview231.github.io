AI in Education: Evidence Before Conclusions

A multi-source analysis comparing what people believe generative AI is doing with measurable evidence about adoption, work, employment, wages, and educational outcomes.

Analytical Approach

Keep competing explanations alive until evidence can separate them.

The portfolio value of this project is the method: joining unlike evidence carefully, documenting limitations, and resisting a predetermined conclusion.

Survey evidence

Measure attitudes without treating them as outcomes.

Student responses show adoption, perceived usefulness, integrity concerns, misinformation risk, and uncertainty about critical-thinking benefits.

Labor evidence

Compare theoretical exposure with actual outcomes.

Occupation exposure can be joined to BLS employment and wage trends to test whether highly exposed occupations actually behaved differently after 2022.

Business evidence

Use adoption data instead of headlines.

Census data supports comparisons by industry, business size, state, and metro area.

Causal discipline

Exposure is not replacement.

A job being technically exposed to an LLM is not evidence that AI eliminated the job. Association and causal attribution remain separate.

Preliminary student evidence

Use and concern can coexist.

71.4% of respondents answering the adoption question reported having used ChatGPT.

63.4% agreed it improves access to knowledge and 59.4% agreed it improves study efficiency.

Only 36.0% agreed it improves critical-thinking skills.

44.9% agreed it encourages cheating, 43.9% cited inaccurate information risk, and 43.5% agreed it encourages plagiarism.

Methodological safeguards

Survey attitudes are not objective outcomes. Correlation is not causation. Incompatible surveys are not pooled simply because they discuss similar topics. Missing evidence should create testable predictions before acquisition whenever possible.
