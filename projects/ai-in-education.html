Research Journey

The project evolved from a narrow question into an evidence system.

The important portfolio signal is not a predetermined conclusion. It is the process used to keep competing explanations alive until the data can separate them.

Phase 1

Sentiment and test-score questions

The project began by asking whether AI-related fears matched educational trends and whether standardized-score changes predated generative AI.

Phase 2

Large-scale student evidence

A 23,218-response higher-education survey created a much stronger foundation for measuring adoption, perceived benefits, integrity concerns, misinformation concerns, and critical-thinking perceptions.

Phase 3

U.S. objective evidence

The scope expanded into Census business adoption, O*NET task structure, OpenAI occupation exposure, and BLS employment/wage outcomes to compare perceptions with measurable changes.

Phase 4

Causality and long-run outcomes

The next layer adds labor churn, verified layoff attribution, business births/deaths, and national test-score histories to test whether AI is being blamed for changes that began earlier or have other causes.

Preliminary Evidence

People appear willing to use AI while remaining uneasy about what it means.

These are descriptive findings from the evidence currently analyzed. They are not final causal conclusions.

Student survey

71.4% of respondents answering the adoption question reported having used ChatGPT.

63.4% agreed it improves access to knowledge and 59.4% agreed it improves study efficiency.

Only 36.0% agreed it improves critical-thinking skills.

44.9% agreed it encourages cheating, 43.9% cited inaccurate information risk, and 43.5% agreed it encourages plagiarism.

Current interpretation

Use and concern can coexist.

The emerging pattern is more nuanced than pro-AI versus anti-AI. Students appear to value access, speed, and practical usefulness while expressing substantially more uncertainty about deep learning, critical thinking, accuracy, and academic integrity.

Next Major Analysis

Does theoretical AI exposure line up with actual job outcomes?

The next joined dataset will connect OpenAI exposure scores and O*NET task structure to BLS OEWS employment and wage data from 2021 through 2025.

Hypothesis A

Displacement

Highly exposed occupations show weaker employment growth or outright losses after 2022.

Hypothesis B

Augmentation

Highly exposed occupations retain or gain employment while wages or productivity improve.

Hypothesis C

Hiring suppression

Employment growth slows without an unusual layoff spike because employers simply hire fewer people.

Hypothesis D

No clear effect yet

AI exposure has little detectable relationship with employment outcomes once ordinary economic variation is considered.

Methodological rule

Exposure is not replacement.

A job being technically exposed to an LLM is not evidence that AI eliminated the job. Employment changes remain associations until labor-flow data, company disclosures, WARN records, and other primary evidence strengthen or weaken the causal story.

Long-run education hypothesis

The education-outcomes track will compare eras including social-media expansion, smartphone dominance, Common Core implementation, COVID disruption, and generative AI. The goal is to test whether earlier changes were associated with larger score shifts than ChatGPT-era changes rather than assuming AI is the cause.
